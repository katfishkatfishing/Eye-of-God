That boy, Khallenghar, stayed after the seminar to ask about Syracuse.

He sat in the wooden armchair across from my desk. I watched him while he talked. It was exhausting just to look at him.

He couldn't stay still. In the span of three minutes, he shifted his weight four times, scratched the side of his nose, adjusted his spectacles,
cleared his throat and tapped his foot against the floorboards. His chest rose and fell twelve times every minute, pulling in air and pushing it
out, burning through fuel, decaying by fractions of a milimeter every day.

He blinks every four seconds. A wet, soft wipe of skin over fluid. 

It makes me sick. His eyes are too clear.

He has no idea how much energy he wastes just keeping his eyes wet. He has no idea how noisy it is to exist like that. Pumping blood through 600000 miles of vessels just to sit in a chair and ask about ancient history.

He glanced down at the corner of my desk blotter.

He asked if that was a catalog number. Aperture-03. I hadn't noticed that was there.

I covered it with my grade ledger and dismissed his question.

My voice was too flat. I have to remember to modulate the inflection when I speak to them. Otherwise, they look at me they way people look at a
clock that ticks a fraction of a second too slowly.
